# The Question of Creation

Existence itself is the first and most undeniable fact; creation is the question we ask when we wonder why this fact is here at all. Religion traditionally claimed authority over the mystery of existence itself: where the universe came from along with its true nature, why it is here, what our and what human life means within it. We are reviewing the creation accounts of the major religions not because we intend to accept them, but because they show how earlier cultures tried to answer the deepest questions with the symbolic, imaginative, and metaphysical tools available to them. Some of these accounts may contain poetic or psychological insight, but as explanations of reality they are limited, culturally conditioned, and often no longer intellectually honest for the modern mind. A coherent religion of the future must therefore take these questions back: not by replacing old dogmas with new dogmas, and not by pretending that science has already answered everything, but by reopening the great questions with seriousness, humility, evidence, reason, and wonder. The question of creation is too profound to be surrendered either to inherited mythology or to a shallow modern indifference.

How the cosmos came into being is a question that still eludes us. The existence of anything at all, rather than a state of total nothingness, fundamentally defies intuition. We possess robust models for the happenings of the early universe, yet no definitive theory for its absolute genesis. When confronted without the comfort of assumed answers, the sheer reality of existence ceases to be merely a scientific puzzle and stands as an immense and profound mystery. How fortunate we are to experience the inexplicable grace of existence. 

The modern mind, shaped by scientific explanation and historical criticism, often finds early cosmogonic myths (creation myths) unsatisfying because they explain the origin of the world through divine personalities, symbolic events, and poetic imagination rather than impersonal laws, evidence, or causal mechanisms. For this reason, such narratives may even be ridiculed as primitive or naïve, even though it is possible that they were meant less as literal science and more of symbolic attempts to express humanity’s place within the mystery of existence. 

## Why Does Anything Exist at All?

 The universe, from the grandest cosmic scales to the most intimate biological processes, is a theatre of perpetual transformation. Nothing truly stands still; everything is in constant flux, ceaselessly becoming. Yet, amidst this ceaseless dynamism, a fundamental order prevails. We observe that new phenomena don’t spontaneously erupt from an absolute void, devoid of any preceding conditions. Similarly, when things cease to be in one form, they don’t vanish into utter nothingness, leaving no imprint or successor. Across countless observations, from the meticulous experiments of science to everyday experience, a foundational principle asserts itself with unwavering consistency: everything arises from what came before, and in turn, everything contributes to what will follow. This unbroken chain of cause and effect, this continuity of existence, forms the very fabric of the physical reality we perceive.

While this principle of causal continuity, that everything arises from what came before and gives rise to what follows, serves us extraordinarily well in understanding the ongoing operations of the universe, it encounters a formidable, perhaps ultimate, challenge when we turn our gaze to the very genesis of existence itself. The question, famously and starkly articulated by the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “Why is there something rather than nothing?” throws our most fundamental assumptions about causality into profound disarray.

If every event, every entity, has a prior cause, then what caused the universe? If we trace the chain of causation backward, from our present experience to the formation of Earth, to the birth of our sun, to the earliest galaxies, and ultimately to the theorized Big Bang itself, we confront a seemingly insurmountable wall. What, or who, initiated that primordial event? This is the classical problem of the First Cause.

The problem of the First Cause, therefore, forces us to interrogate the limits of our understanding of causality, time, and even the nature of explanation itself. Is an infinite regress truly illogical, or merely counter-intuitive to minds evolved to deal with finite sequences? Does our concept of “cause,” forged in experiences within the universe, even apply to the universe’s origin, where space and time as we understand them may break down? Or, as some ponder, is the very question “Why is there something rather than nothing?” predicated on human cognitive biases and linguistic structures that may not map onto ultimate reality? While scientific inquiry relentlessly pushes back the frontiers of the known, this deepest of questions continues to shimmer at the edge of comprehension, a testament to both the ambition of the human intellect and the profound mystery of existence.

## Rig Veda

One of the oldest accounts of creation, found in the Rig Veda (the foundational scripture of Hinduism and one of the oldest surviving texts in any Indo-European language, composed orally around 1500 BCE), is actually breathtakingly agnostic and contemplative:

### Nasadiya Sukta (Rig Veda 10.129.1-7)

Non-being then existed not nor being: [More modern translations phrase this as "Then, there was neither existence, nor non-existence."]\
There was no air, nor sky that is beyond it.\
What was concealed? Wherein? In whose protection?\
And was there deep unfathomable water?

Death then existed not nor life immortal;\
Of neither night nor day was any token.\
By its inherent force the One breathed windless:\
No other thing than that beyond existed.

Darkness there was at first by darkness hidden;\
Without distinctive marks, this all was water.\
That which, becoming, by the void was covered,\
That One by force of heat came into being.

Desire entered the One in the beginning:\
It was the earliest seed, of thought the product.\
The sages searching in their hearts with wisdom,\
Found out the bond of being in non-being.

Their ray extended light across the darkness:\
But was the One above or was it under?\
Creative force was there, and fertile power:\
Below was energy, above was impulse.

Who knows for certain? Who shall here declare it?\
Whence was it born, and whence came this creation?\
The gods were born after this world's creation:\
Then who can know from whence it has arisen?

None knoweth whence creation has arisen;\
And whether he has or has not produced it:\
He who surveys it in the highest heaven,\
He only knows, or haply he may know not.

Here the First Cause is described as a state of neither existence nor non-existence [@Macdonell1917vedic, p. 207]. The human mind tends to categorizes everything as either existing or not existing (although modern physics might perhaps allow for new states outside of this dichotomoy). You can also say it's neither existence or non-existence and something deeper than or beyond both, or that putting things into two simple categories is likely an oversimplification hiding a continuum, but that's something we will get into later in this treatise. By saying the origin of the universe is neither, the poet is implying that human logic is too small to grasp the First Cause.

It is a conceptually ambiguous hymn because it does not provide an answer; instead, it strips away assumptions. It suggests that the origin of reality is so profoundly mysterious that any definitive claim about it is automatically false. 

The conceptual ambiguity of this hymn is contradicted elsewhere in the Rig Veda. In fact, the Rig Veda is filled with very positive, definitive, and highly graphic creation myths. It does not speak with a single, unified voice.

Scholars often note that the Rig Veda contains a range of cognitive styles, from richly anthropomorphic and mythic imagery to more abstract and speculative reflection. While earlier hymns frequently express cosmology through divine and symbolic narratives, the later Nasadiya Sukta (10.129) stands out for its radical ambiguity and epistemological restraint. Rather than asserting a clear origin, it questions whether such knowledge is even accessible. 

## Prasthāna-Traya

The prasthāna-traya refers to three foundational texts:

* The Upaniṣads (the end portions of the Vedas)
* The Brahma Sutras
* The Bhagavad Gita

The prasthāna-traya does address cosmogony and cosmology, but not in the way a modern scientific or even systematic philosophical framework would.  Its treatments are varied, sometimes inconsistent on the surface, and ultimately subordinated to a deeper concern: the nature of reality and the self.

### Upaniṣads

The Upaniṣads offer a range of speculative reflections that increasingly shift attention from external, ritualized descriptions of the cosmos toward the underlying nature of reality and the self.

Rather than positing a strict separation between creator and creation, many Upaniṣadic passages describe the universe as emerging from, or grounded in, a single ultimate principle: Brahman. This emergence is expressed through diverse metaphors: emanation (as in the spider and its web in the Mundaka Upanishad), intentional self-differentiation (“may I be many” in the Chandogya Upanishad), or graded manifestation (as in elemental sequences found in texts such as the Taittiriya Upanishad). These accounts are not fully harmonized and are best understood as complementary symbolic models rather than literal cosmological theories.

A central and recurring theme is that the essence of the cosmos and the essence of the self are intimately related. Teachings such as “tat tvam asi” (“thou are that”) express the idea that ultimate reality is not merely the origin of the universe but is also accessible through inward inquiry. In this sense, the Upaniṣads shift the emphasis from explaining the structure of the external world to realizing the ground of both subject and object.

Later interpreters, especially Adi Shankaracharya, systematized these insights into a nondual framework in which the empirical world is understood as a dependent or provisional reality grounded in Brahman. However, the Upaniṣads themselves remain plural in voice, combining cosmological speculation with a sustained inquiry into the nature of ultimate reality.

### Brahma Sutras

Cosmogony and cosmology play a significant role in the Brahma Sutras, but they are not treated as ends in themselves. Rather, they are integral to the text’s primary aim: to interpret and systematize the diverse statements about Brahman found in the Upaniṣads.

The opening definition, “janmādy asya yataḥ” (1.1.2), identifies Brahman as that from which the origin, sustenance, and dissolution of the universe proceed. In this way, cosmological reflection becomes a means of characterizing the nature of ultimate reality through its relation to the world.

A central concern of the text is the causal status of Brahman. The sutras argue, on the basis of Upaniṣadic passages, that Brahman is the source of the universe, while engaging in extended debate with rival philosophical systems such as Sāṅkhya and Vaiśeṣika, which posit unconscious material principles or atomic constituents as the basis of the cosmos. These discussions are not purely speculative but are tied to questions of scriptural interpretation and metaphysical coherence.

The Brahma Sutras also address apparent inconsistencies among Upaniṣadic creation accounts, attempting to reconcile differing descriptions of cosmological emergence. However, the text does not present a single, fully elaborated cosmological model; its aphoristic nature leaves room for divergent interpretations, which later Vedānta traditions develop in distinct ways.

Ultimately, cosmology in the Brahma Sutras serves a philosophical purpose: to establish Brahman as the fundamental reality underlying the universe. The precise nature of the relationship between Brahman and the world (whether understood in nondual, qualified, or dual terms)is worked out in the subsequent commentarial traditions, most influentially by Adi Shankaracharya and other Vedānta thinkers.

### Bhagavad Gita

The Gītā presents a vision of the universe grounded in a supreme reality identified with Krishna, who is described as both the source and sustaining principle of the cosmos. Passages describing the periodic creation and dissolution of the world situate human life within a vast, cyclical cosmic process. At the same time, the text draws on conceptual frameworks associated with Sāṅkhya, such as the distinction between puruṣa (consciousness) and prakṛti (material nature), as well as Upaniṣadic ideas concerning the identity or relation between the self and ultimate reality.

These cosmological elements are not presented as a single, systematic doctrine. Rather, they serve to orient the practitioner: to contextualize action, support detachment, and frame devotion. The universe is portrayed as pervaded and sustained by the divine, yet not reducible to a simple metaphysical formula, allowing for multiple interpretive possibilities.

In this way, cosmology in the Gītā functions less as an explanatory system and more as a theological and philosophical backdrop for its central concern: the transformation of human understanding and conduct in relation to ultimate reality.

## Advaita Vedānta

Advaita Vedānta is an extension of the wisdom found in the prasthāna-traya and other sources. It is primarily concerned with the removal of ignorance and the direct realization of one’s true nature (that is, the nature of the self (Ātman) and its identity with ultimate reality (Brahman)). Questions of cosmological origin are treated as secondary and ultimately provisional. Rather than seeking answers through speculative cosmology, the tradition emphasizes direct realization through inward investigation.

Adi Shankaracharya did not originate Advaita Vedānta but systematized and established it as a major philosophical tradition through his commentaries on the prasthāna-traya. While he engages extensively with cosmogonic and cosmological statements found in the Upaniṣads and the Brahma Sutras, he interprets them as provisional teachings situated at the level of empirical reality. For Śaṅkara, ultimate reality (Brahman) is nondual and changeless, and the world of multiplicity is an appearance dependent on ignorance (avidyā). As such, cosmology is not denied but relativized: it serves as a pedagogical framework that is ultimately sublated by the realization of the identity of Ātman and Brahman.

While cosmogony asks what the origin of the universe is, Advaita Vedānta asks who is asking this question.

## Vedic Literature

While the prasthāna-traya occupies a foundational role within the Vedānta tradition, it represents only a selective consolidation of themes drawn from a much broader and internally diverse textual corpus.

The wider Vedic literature includes the Saṃhitās, Brāhmaṇas, and Āraṇyakas, alongside a large number of later Upaniṣads of varying historical and philosophical significance. Beyond these, extensive cosmological, ritual, and metaphysical reflections are developed in other genres, such as the Purāṇas, as well as in the sūtra literature and subsequent commentarial traditions associated with multiple schools of Indian philosophy. Some consider the much more recent teachings of Ramana Maharshi the clearest exposition ever provided in this territory.

It is important, however, to distinguish between these bodies of literature. Texts such as the Yoga Sutras and the Mimamsa Sutras belong to distinct philosophical systems with their own priorities and methods, while later works such as the Vivekachudamani reflect specific developments within Advaita Vedānta. As such, the broader corpus does not constitute a single, unified framework, but rather a plurality of interpretive traditions engaging overlapping questions about reality, knowledge, and liberation.

In this context, the prasthāna-traya is best understood not as a comprehensive summary of the entire tradition, but as a focal point for Vedāntic inquiry. Its study allows for the examination of key metaphysical and theological concerns, while the wider textual landscape remains essential for understanding the diversity and historical development of Indian philosophical thought.

## Ancient Near Eastern (Mesopotamian, Egyptian)

Ancient Near Eastern and Egyptian cosmogonies are generally not formulated in terms of creatio ex nihilo (creation out of nothing), a concept that emerges more explicitly in later Hellenistic and rabbinic thought. Instead, these traditions typically presuppose some form of pre-existing, undifferentiated condition, often, though not exclusively, represented as primordial waters, from which ordered reality is established. In this context, creation is frequently depicted as the imposition of structure, differentiation, and stability rather than the absolute origination of matter [@Walton2006ancient].

In Mesopotamian sources, most notably the Enuma Elish, cosmogony is closely associated with divine conflict. The defeat of the primordial figure Tiamat by the god Marduk provides the basis for cosmic formation, as the ordered world is constructed from her divided body. The creation of humanity, formed from the blood of the rebel figure Kingu, is presented in functional terms: humans assume labor and ritual responsibilities that sustain the divine order. These elements together suggest a cosmology in which order is established through divine sovereignty and maintained through ongoing ritual and social practice [@Dalley2000myths; @Walton2006ancient].

Egyptian cosmogonies, by contrast, are preserved in multiple, regionally distinct theological traditions, including the Heliopolitan and Memphite systems. These accounts typically begin with a primordial condition associated with the waters of Nun, from which differentiated existence emerges. The mechanisms of creation vary: some traditions describe forms of physical generation, as in the self-engendering activity of Atum, while others, such as the Memphite theology preserved on the Shabaka Stone, emphasize creation through thought and speech, attributed to the god Ptah. Despite these differences, a recurring theme is the establishment of ma’at—a principle encompassing order, balance, and stability—which defines and sustains the created world in contrast to the surrounding undifferentiated state [@Allen1988genesis; @Hornung1982conceptions].

## Ancient Greek Religion and Philosophy

The development of classical Greek philosophy from earlier Greek religious traditions is often described as a movement from mythos (narrative myth) to logos (rational account). While this framing captures a genuine shift in emphasis, scholars increasingly understand it not as a clean rupture but as a gradual transformation, in which mythic modes of thought are reinterpreted and systematized into more abstract and analytical forms of explanation [@Morgan2000myth].

The mythic baseline of this tradition is codified in Theogony (c. 8th century BCE). Like many Ancient Near Eastern cosmologies, Hesiod does not posit creatio ex nihilo. The cosmos begins with Khaos—not “disorder” in the modern sense, but a yawning gap or void—from which emerges a genealogical succession of primordial entities, including Gaia (Earth), Tartarus (the abyss), and Eros (desire). Creation unfolds through processes of generation and succession, often marked by conflict, as in the overthrow of Ouranos by Cronus and of Cronus by Zeus. These narratives can be understood as articulating the emergence and stabilization of cosmic order, though their meanings are not reducible to a single political or moral framework [@Caldwell1987origin].

A significant reorientation begins in the 6th century BCE with the Pre-Socratic philosophers of Ionia, who sought to identify underlying principles (archai) governing the natural world. Rather than relying primarily on divine genealogies, thinkers such as Thales, Anaximander, and Heraclitus proposed more abstract accounts of cosmic order. Thales identified water as a fundamental substrate, while Anaximander posited the apeiron, an indefinite and boundless source from which opposites emerge. Heraclitus introduced the concept of the logos, an underlying principle of order and intelligibility governing the perpetual flux of the cosmos, though not in the sense of fully explicit or mechanistic rationality [@Kirk1983presocratic].

By the classical period, Plato presents a highly developed cosmology in the Timaeus. Here, a divine craftsman (Demiurge) orders a pre-existing, chaotic receptacle (chōra) according to eternal Forms (eide). The cosmos is thus structured as an intelligible and purposive system, endowed with a world-soul and oriented toward the realization of order and goodness, though the Demiurge operates within constraints rather than creating ex nihilo [@Cornford1937platos].

Aristotle further transforms Greek cosmology by arguing for an eternal, ungenerated universe. Rejecting temporal accounts of creation, he posits a cosmos structured through eternal motion, organized into concentric spheres. At its apex is the Unmoved Mover, which functions not as an efficient cause but as a final cause—an object of desire and thought that accounts for the ordered, continuous motion of the heavens [@Guthrie1981history].

## Zoroastrianism

Although sometimes treated as peripheral in broad historical surveys, Zoroastrianism is often regarded as an important intermediary tradition in the development of ancient cosmological and eschatological thought. Its worldview is structured around a pronounced dualism, articulated as a conflict between asha (truth, order, righteousness) and druj (falsehood, disorder, deception). In the earliest strata of the tradition, particularly the Gathas, this dualism is expressed in more ethical and theological terms, while later texts, including the Avesta and Pahlavi works such as the Bundahishn, develop more elaborate cosmological formulations. In these accounts, the supreme deity Ahura Mazda creates the material world as the arena in which the destructive force Angra Mainyu (Ahriman) is ultimately confronted and overcome. Accordingly, the physical universe is understood as fundamentally good and purposeful, though presently subject to corruption through the intrusion of destructive forces [@Boyce2001zoroastrians].

A distinctive feature of Zoroastrian cosmology is its emphasis on a structured, directional conception of time. In contrast to cyclical models found in many Indian traditions, and to the eternal cosmology of Aristotle, Zoroastrian texts describe a temporal framework with a defined beginning and a projected culmination. This process leads toward the Frashokereti, the final renovation of the cosmos, in which evil is eliminated, the dead are resurrected, and creation is restored to a perfected state. Human beings play an active role in this process, as moral agents whose thoughts, words, and deeds contribute to the ongoing realization of cosmic order [@Zaehner1961dawn].

The historical significance of these ideas remains a subject of scholarly discussion. Many researchers have proposed that, during periods of Persian imperial influence, elements of Zoroastrian cosmology (particularly linear eschatology, final judgment, and resurrection), may have interacted with and influenced developments within Second Temple Judaism. Others, however, emphasize the complexity of these exchanges and caution against assuming direct or unidirectional transmission. While clear lines of influence are difficult to establish with certainty, the thematic parallels between Zoroastrian, Jewish, Christian, and Islamic eschatological traditions continue to be an important area of comparative study [@Cohn1993cosmos].

## Early Chinese traditions

Early Chinese cosmologies represent a significant departure from many Mediterranean paradigms, as they generally do not center on the concept of an external creator deity or a divine artisan imposing form onto inert matter. Instead, scholars often characterize early Chinese thought as presenting an “organismic” or process-relational cosmos: a reality understood as self-generating, dynamic, and continuously unfolding, rather than as a created artifact [@Graham1989disputers].

The most explicitly cosmogonic reflections within this tradition appear in early Daoist texts such as the Daodejing and the Zhuangzi. In these works, the origin and sustaining principle of the cosmos is the Dao (the Way)—an ineffable and inexhaustible source that underlies all phenomena. The Dao is not typically understood as a conscious creator, but rather as the generative process through which reality unfolds. This process is often expressed through the sequence found in Daodejing 42: the Dao gives rise to the One, the One to the Two (yin and yang), the Two to the Three, and from this dynamic interplay emerge the “ten thousand things.” This unfolding occurs through ziran (natural spontaneity) and wuwei (non-coercive action), suggesting a cosmos that develops according to its own intrinsic patterns rather than toward a fixed, externally imposed teleological endpoint [@Ames2003daodejing].

In contrast, early Confucian (Ruist) traditions tend to bracket questions of ultimate cosmogonic origin and instead focus on cosmology as a moral and relational order. For thinkers such as Confucius, Mencius, and Xunzi, the central concept is Tian (Heaven), which by the classical period had come to signify a normative cosmic order that is not consistently anthropomorphic, yet retains moral authority. Confucian cosmology envisions a tripartite relationship between Heaven, Earth, and Humanity, in which human beings play an active and indispensable role. Through the cultivation of virtue (de) and the performance of ritual (li), humans participate in and complete the processes of cosmic harmony—an orientation often described by scholars as “anthropocosmic” [@Tu1989centrality].

A further development that connects Daoist and Confucian perspectives is the emergence of correlative cosmology, articulated in texts such as the I Ching and elaborated during the Han dynasty. This framework understands the universe as an interconnected web of resonances (ganying), structured through the complementary dynamics of yin and yang and the cyclical interactions of the Five Phases (wuxing: wood, fire, earth, metal, water). Rather than emphasizing linear causation or material reduction, this system highlights patterns of correspondence between different domains of reality, linking the human body, social order, and cosmos through analogous processes. This correlative mode of thought became a deeply influential framework within later Chinese intellectual history [@Wang2012yinyang]. 

## Buddhism

Buddhist cosmology differs from many other classical traditions in that it does not place primary emphasis on a definitive cosmogony or a singular act of creation. Instead, it is structured around the principle of Pratītyasamutpāda, which holds that all phenomena arise in dependence upon causes and conditions. Within this framework, the search for an ultimate first cause is not a central concern. Early Buddhist texts record that Gautama Buddha declined to answer questions about the temporal beginning of the universe, classifying them among the Avyākata—issues regarded as not conducive to liberation from suffering [@Gethin1998foundations].

Temporally, many Buddhist traditions describe the cosmos as beginningless and cyclical, unfolding across vast periods known as kalpas (eons). Texts such as the Aggañña Sutta, along with later Abhidharma literature, present models in which world systems arise, endure, decay, and are eventually destroyed, often through elemental forces. These cycles then recur over immeasurable durations. While karma (intentional action) plays a central role in conditioning the experiences and rebirths of sentient beings within these cycles, it is more accurately understood as shaping participation in the cosmos rather than serving as a singular causal mechanism for the creation of the universe itself [@Sadakata1997buddhist].

Spatially, classical Buddhist cosmology describes a multi-layered universe often organized around Mount Meru and structured into the Tridhātu: the Realm of Desire (Kāmadhātu), the Realm of Form (Rūpadhātu), and the Formless Realm (Ārūpyadhātu). These domains correspond not only to different modes of existence but also to varying psychological and ethical conditions. In this sense, cosmology and soteriology are closely linked, as the moral and mental states of beings influence their position within the broader cosmological order. However, interpretations of these structures vary across Buddhist traditions, and some modern perspectives read them in more symbolic or phenomenological terms [@Harvey2013introduction].

From a comparative perspective, Buddhist cosmology is significant in that it articulates a highly structured and ethically inflected vision of reality without positing a creator deity. Instead, it presents a process-oriented model in which the universe is understood through interdependence, impermanence, and the continuity of causal relations. For this reason, it is often examined in comparative philosophy of religion as an alternative to both theistic and strictly materialist cosmologies [@Lopez2008buddhism].

## Abrahamic Traditions

The term “Abrahamic traditions” designates a family of monotheistic religions, primarily Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, that trace aspects of their theological and narrative heritage to the figure of Abraham. While each tradition develops distinct doctrinal frameworks, they share a broadly similar cosmological orientation centered on a single, transcendent, personal deity who creates the universe and reveals divine will through historical events and sacred texts.

A defining feature of these traditions is the ontological distinction between Creator and creation. In contrast to pantheistic or emanationist models found in some Indian and Chinese traditions, God is not understood as identical with the material universe or as an extension of it. Rather, the cosmos is contingent upon divine will. These traditions also tend to articulate a directional understanding of time, in which the universe has a beginning, unfolds through a meaningful historical process shaped by revelation, and moves toward an eschatological culmination, often involving judgment and restoration [@Burrell1993freedom].

Historically, however, the articulation of creation within these traditions develops over time. Many modern scholars argue that the earliest biblical texts, such as the opening of Genesis, reflect older Ancient Near Eastern cosmological patterns in which creation is depicted as the ordering of a pre-existing, formless condition associated with the tehom (“the deep”). These accounts may retain echoes of chaoskampf imagery, in which divine power is expressed through the establishment of order over primordial waters [@Levenson1988creation].

In later periods, particularly in dialogue with Hellenistic philosophy, Jewish and Christian thinkers more explicitly formulated the doctrine of creatio ex nihilo, affirming that God brought the entirety of reality, both form and matter, into being without reliance on any pre-existing substrate. This development unfolded gradually and became a central theological commitment in classical Jewish, Christian, and many Islamic theological traditions [@May1994creatio].

Within these traditions, the doctrine of creation takes diverse forms. In Jewish thought, creation is often associated with the divine word; in Christian theology, it is articulated through the concept of the Logos; and in certain schools of Islamic kalām, particularly the Ash‘arite tradition, divine omnipotence is emphasized through the idea that God continuously sustains or recreates the world at every moment. Despite these differences, a common theme is the affirmation of the created world as meaningful and purposive, serving as the arena for moral action and the unfolding of the relationship between the divine and humanity.

## Indigenous and Animistic Traditions 

Categorizing the cosmologies of Indigenous and animistic traditions globally presents a significant scholarly challenge. These traditions are highly diverse, locally grounded, and often transmitted through oral, ritual, and embodied practices rather than centralized textual canons. Rather than lacking systematization, they frequently encode complex philosophical and ecological knowledge in narrative, ceremony, and land-based relationships. Contemporary scholars associated with the “new animism,” such as Graham Harvey, have identified recurring patterns across many such traditions, describing them as relational ontologies in which the world is understood as a network of interacting “persons,” only some of whom are human [@Harvey2005animism].

Within this interpretive framework, the cosmos is often understood not as inert matter created by a distant, transcendent deity, but as an animate and socially embedded environment in which meaning emerges through kinship, reciprocity, and place-based relationships. However, these patterns should not be taken as universal, as Indigenous cosmologies vary significantly in their metaphysical assumptions and symbolic structures.

In the Americas, many traditions articulate cosmogony through cooperative or emergent processes. The “Earth-diver” motif, in which animal beings such as muskrat or turtle retrieve material from primordial waters to form the land, is widespread across parts of North America, though not universal. Vine Deloria Jr. influentially argued that many Native American traditions emphasize the primacy of place over abstract historical time, suggesting that the sacred is anchored in specific landscapes and continually enacted through human responsibility and reciprocal relationships [@Deloria1994god]. In the Andean region, particularly among Quechua and Aymara traditions, cosmology often centers on entities such as Pachamama and principles like yanantin (complementary dualism), where balance is maintained through ongoing exchange between humans, landforms, and celestial forces.

In Oceania, Aboriginal Australian traditions offer highly developed and diverse cosmological frameworks often referred to (imprecisely) as the Dreaming. Rather than denoting a single, uniform doctrine, this term encompasses a range of regionally specific systems. As described by W. E. H. Stanner, the Dreaming can be understood as an “Everywhen,” an ongoing reality in which ancestral beings shape and sustain the world. These beings are often said to have moved through a pre-existing environment, giving form, meaning, and law to the landscape, with their presence continuing to inhere in specific sites. Ritual practices are therefore not merely commemorative but are understood as actively maintaining the continuity of the world.

Across Africa, many traditional religions describe a layered cosmos involving a creator deity, ancestral presences, and a variety of spiritual forces. In some interpretations, influenced by scholars such as John Mbiti, the creator may be seen as relatively remote after the initial act of creation, though this characterization does not apply uniformly across all traditions. Greater emphasis is often placed on ancestors and localized spiritual agencies, as well as on concepts of vital force that animate both human and non-human life. In parts of Northern Eurasia and Inner Asia, cosmological systems often include multiple interconnected realms—commonly described as upper, middle, and lower worlds—linked by a central axis such as a world tree. This structure, classically analyzed by Mircea Eliade, is associated with shamanic practices in which ritual specialists mediate between realms to restore balance and address illness understood in cosmological terms [@Eliade1964shamanism].
